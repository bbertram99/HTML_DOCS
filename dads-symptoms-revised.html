Part 3: Angle #1 to Consider — Blood Flow After Meals

Could the Lower Aorta Narrowing Help Explain the Post-Meal Stomach Pain & Leg Pain?

Two of Dad’s most consistent symptoms are: (1) sharp abdominal pain and diarrhea occurring 30 to 90 minutes after eating (which improved when he fasted before his PET scan), and (2) deep pain in his right thigh and lower leg, along with getting winded on stairs.

The Physiological Connection: Post-Meal Blood Flow Demand

The lower (abdominal) aorta is the main highway carrying blood through the belly before branching into the intestines (via the mesenteric arteries) and splitting into the left and right legs (via the iliac arteries).

  • Why 30–90 Minutes After Eating Matters: Published vascular research shows that within minutes of eating, the digestive tract requires a large surge in blood flow for the next 1–3+ hours to digest food. When plaque narrows the lower aorta or its digestive branches, the gut may not receive enough blood flow during digestion—causing the bowel muscle to cramp sharply and trigger diarrhea (a condition called Chronic Mesenteric Ischemia or “intestinal angina”).
  • Why Fasting Before the PET Scan Helped: Fasting meant his digestive tract didn’t have to ask for extra blood flow, allowing the gut cramps to settle.
  • The Right Leg Connection: Narrowing in the lower aorta where it branches into the legs (Aortoiliac Occlusive Disease) commonly causes deep aching or pain in the thigh and lower leg, especially with activity.
  • Why Crestor & Telmisartan Are Helpful Here: Even if blood cholesterol numbers look okay, doctors prescribe statins like Crestor and blood pressure medications like Telmisartan to stabilize plaque in the lower aorta and keep those vessels protected.

Part 4: Angle #2 to Consider — Sleep Aids & Dry Eyes

How Nighttime Sleep Aids Can Affect Dry Eyes, Morning Energy & Other Meds

Getting a solid night’s sleep is huge when dealing with chronic pain. After the sleep specialist stopped high-dose Zopiclone (15 mg) about 8 months ago, Dad understandably looked for over-the-counter ways to sleep—using Nytol 50 mg, Melatonin 10–20 mg, and CBD/THC oil, while also recently restarting Serc 24 mg at night.

1. Why Eye Compresses Have a Hard Time Working Alongside Nytol 50 mg

The hospital ophthalmologist found that Dad’s eyelid oil glands (Meibomian glands) have low secretion, causing dry eyes, sensitivity to bright light, and headaches. They recommended warm compresses twice a day and artificial tears.

The practical problem: Nytol 50 mg (diphenhydramine) has strong anticholinergic (drying) effects—meaning it temporarily blocks the nerve signals that tell the eyes to produce tears and protective oils. High doses of THC can also reduce tear production. That makes it much harder for warm compresses to keep up. Gently stepping down off Nytol gives the ophthalmologist’s warm compress routine a real chance to work. Chronic dry eyes themselves can produce eye pain and headaches—both of which Dad experiences.

📚 Source #3: Ophthalmology Review — How Antihistamines/Sleep Aids Dry Out Eye Glands

Ophthalmology Reference: Medications That Cause or Worsen Dry Eye Syndrome

"Antihistamines such as diphenhydramine (found in nighttime sleep aids) block muscarinic receptors on the lacrimal and meibomian glands, significantly reducing both watery tear production and oil secretion, worsening evaporative dry eye, light sensitivity, and ocular headaches."
Read Ophthalmology Article →

2. Taking Serc (24 mg) and Nytol (50 mg) at the Same Time at Night

Dad takes Serc (betahistine) 24 mg at night because it causes drowsiness, along with Nytol (diphenhydramine) 50 mg. In pharmacy reference guides, these two medications work in opposite directions on the exact same receptor:

  • Serc (Betahistine) stimulates Histamine H1 receptors.
  • Nytol (Diphenhydramine) blocks Histamine H1 receptors.

Because they push and pull on the same receptor, taking Nytol alongside Serc can reduce how well Serc works while increasing next-day drowsiness and dry mouth.

Part 5: Angle #3 to Consider — Brief 30–60 Second Stabbing Pains

Why Do “Knife-Stab” Pains Last 30–60 Seconds—And Why Does a Small Amount of Alcohol Sometimes Calm Them?

While reduced blood flow in the lower aorta makes sense for the stomach and leg, it doesn’t fully explain why Dad gets sudden, 30-to-60-second “knife-stabbing” pains in his mid-chest (where taking a deep breath or pressing hurts), right ear, or other sites—or why a small amount of hard alcohol sometimes helps when 1000 mg of Tylenol does not.

1. How Pain Nerves Can “Misfire” in Short Bursts

In neurology, sharp stabbing pain that switches on for 30 to 60 seconds and then fades is characteristic of small-fiber nerve hyper-excitability (neuralgia). When small nerves between the ribs (intercostal nerves) misfire, they can briefly cramp the tiny rib muscles—making a deep breath, cough, or touch hurt sharply until the burst passes. These attacks can start and stop while sitting or lying completely still.

2. Possible Contributing Factors (Hypothesis Only)

Looking back at Dad’s history, a few things can interact with the nervous system’s natural “dimmer switch” (GABA-A receptors):

  • Prior Cipro / Fluoroquinolone Antibiotic: Medical literature shows fluoroquinolones can sensitize small peripheral nerves and temporarily affect GABA-A receptors.
  • Stopping High-Dose Zopiclone (15 mg) 8 Months Ago: Zopiclone works by pressing down on GABA-A receptors at night. After long-term high-dose use and abrupt stop, some people experience a period of increased nerve sensitivity. How long this lasts varies widely between individuals.
  • Why a Small Amount of Alcohol Sometimes Helps: Alcohol also acts on GABA-A receptors and can dampen certain pain signals. This is a temporary effect only and is not a recommended long-term strategy.

Important: These are possible contributing factors, not proven causes of the multi-year pattern. The short duration of each attack remains the most useful clinical clue.

3. A Quick Note on the B-100 Complex

Dad takes Nature’s Bounty B-100 off and on because a doctor noted borderline-low Vitamin B12. A standard B-100 tablet supplies B12 but also contains 100 mg of Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine) — roughly 60 times the normal daily requirement (1.3–1.7 mg).

Vitamin B6 has a long half-life in nerve tissue (often 15–30 days). Taking 100 mg even intermittently over months or years can still allow levels to build up. Health agencies have linked high-strength B6 supplements to sensory nerve irritation in some people.

B12 itself does not cause this problem. Switching to a plain Vitamin B12 tablet (e.g., 1,000 mcg) gives him the B12 he needs without the large extra dose of B6. If other supplements are also being taken, it is worth checking their B6 content as well.
